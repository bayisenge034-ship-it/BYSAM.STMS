Biology

Study notes, labelled diagrams, practical work and exam practice for secondary-school Biology.

Printable copy: Download Biology notes + exercises (PDF)

1. Cells and organisation

All living things are made of cells. Cells group into tissues, tissues into organs, organs into organ systems, and systems into an organism.

OrganelleFunction
Nucleuscontains DNA; controls cell activities
Mitochondriaaerobic respiration releases energy
Ribosomesmake proteins
Cell membranecontrols what enters and leaves
Chloroplastphotosynthesis (plants only)
Worked example – magnification
Magnification = image size ÷ actual size. A cell drawn 30 mm long that is really 0.15 mm long: 30 ÷ 0.15 = ×200. (Convert to the same units first.)

2. Movement of substances

  • Diffusion: net movement of particles from high to low concentration (e.g. oxygen into cells).
  • Osmosis: net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane from a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to lower water potential (concentrated solution).
  • Active transport: movement against a concentration gradient using energy from respiration (e.g. mineral ions into root hairs).

In pure water, plant cells absorb water and become turgid (firm); in strong solution they lose water and become flaccid, and the membrane may pull away from the wall (plasmolysis). Animal cells burst in pure water and shrivel in concentrated solution.

Worked example – surface area to volume
A cube of side 2 cm: surface area = 6 × 4 = 24 cm², volume = 8 cm³, ratio 3 : 1. Side 4 cm: 96 cm² and 64 cm³, ratio 1.5 : 1. Smaller objects have a larger ratio, so exchange (e.g. of oxygen) is faster – why large organisms need specialised exchange surfaces such as lungs.

3. Nutrition, digestion and enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts made of protein. Each fits one substrate (lock-and-key). Rate rises with temperature up to an optimum (about 37 °C in humans), then falls as the enzyme denatures – its active site changes shape permanently. pH also has an optimum.

EnzymeSubstrate → productsMade / works in
Amylasestarch → maltosesalivary glands, pancreas
Proteaseprotein → amino acidsstomach, pancreas
Lipasefats → fatty acids + glycerolpancreas, small intestine

Food tests: iodine solution – starch gives blue-black; Benedict's solution (heat) – reducing sugar gives green → yellow → brick-red precipitate; biuret – protein gives purple; ethanol emulsion test – fats give a cloudy white layer.

4. Respiration

Aerobic: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy
Anaerobic (yeast): glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + a little energy
Anaerobic (muscle): glucose → lactic acid + a little energy

Respiration happens in all living cells all the time. It is not the same as breathing (gas exchange). Aerobic respiration releases far more energy per glucose than anaerobic respiration. After hard exercise you keep breathing deeply to repay the oxygen debt and remove lactic acid.

5. Photosynthesis

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ (needs light energy absorbed by chlorophyll)
  • Limiting factors: light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature. Whichever is in shortest supply limits the rate.
  • Glucose is used for respiration, converted to starch for storage, cellulose for cell walls, or combined with nitrate to make proteins.
  • Starch test on a leaf: boil in water (kills the leaf), boil in ethanol (removes chlorophyll), rinse, add iodine – blue-black shows starch was made.

6. Circulation and the heart

Humans have a double circulation: right side pumps blood to the lungs, left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body. Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure (thick, muscular, elastic walls); veins return blood at low pressure (thinner walls, valves); capillaries are one cell thick for exchange. Blood contains plasma, red cells (haemoglobin carries oxygen), white cells (defence) and platelets (clotting).

7. Reproduction, inheritance and genetics

Genes are sections of DNA that code for proteins. Alleles are different versions of a gene. Dominant alleles (capital letter) show when present; recessive alleles (lower case) show only if two copies are inherited. Genotype is the alleles; phenotype is the visible trait.

Worked example – monohybrid cross
Tall (T) is dominant to short (t). Cross two heterozygous plants, Tt × Tt:
Tt
TTTTt
tTttt
Genotypes 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. Phenotypes 3 tall : 1 short; probability of a short plant = 25%.

Mitosis makes identical cells for growth and repair; meiosis makes gametes with half the chromosomes (humans: 23 in each gamete, 46 in body cells), creating variation.

8. Ecology

A food chain shows energy transfer: producer → primary consumer → secondary consumer. Only about 10% of energy passes to the next level; the rest is lost as heat, movement, waste and uneaten parts, which is why food chains are short.

Worked example
Producers store 8000 kJ. Primary consumers receive about 800 kJ and secondary consumers about 80 kJ.

Humans affect ecosystems through deforestation, pollution and overfishing; conservation includes protected areas, sustainable farming and replanting.

9. Practical activities

  • Osmosis in potato: cut equal potato cylinders, weigh them, place in sugar solutions of different concentrations for 30 minutes, blot and reweigh. Plot % change in mass against concentration; where the line crosses zero is the concentration of the potato cell sap.
  • Effect of temperature on amylase: mix starch and amylase at different temperatures; test samples with iodine every minute. The time for iodine to stop turning blue-black measures the rate.
  • Photosynthesis in pondweed: count oxygen bubbles per minute at different distances from a lamp; keep temperature constant using a water bath.
  • Variables: change one (independent), measure one (dependent), keep the rest the same (controlled). Repeat for reliability.

10. Exercises with answers

  1. A cell appears 45 mm long in a drawing; its real length is 0.3 mm. Find the magnification.
    Answer45 ÷ 0.3 = ×150.
  2. In pea plants, B (tall) is dominant. What are the expected offspring of Bb × bb?
    Answer1 Bb : 1 bb, so 50% tall and 50% short.
  3. Name the organelle where aerobic respiration takes place.
    AnswerMitochondria.
  4. Explain why a wilted lettuce leaf becomes crisp when placed in water.
    AnswerWater enters the cells by osmosis (water potential is higher outside), so vacuoles swell and press on the cell walls; the cells become turgid and support the leaf.
  5. Explain why enzyme activity falls sharply above about 45 °C.
    AnswerHigh temperature breaks bonds that hold the enzyme's shape; the active site changes shape (denatures) so the substrate no longer fits.
  6. 8000 kJ of energy enters the producers. Using the 10% rule, how much reaches secondary consumers?
    Answer8000 → 800 → 80 kJ.
  7. Describe the test for reducing sugar.
    AnswerAdd Benedict's solution and heat in a water bath; a brick-red (orange) precipitate shows a reducing sugar such as glucose.
  8. Give two differences between arteries and veins.
    AnswerArteries have thick, muscular, elastic walls and carry blood away from the heart at high pressure; veins have thinner walls, valves and carry blood to the heart at low pressure.

11. Examination tips

  • Use the command word: state (short fact), describe (what happens), explain (why, using biology), compare (similarities and differences).
  • Draw large, clear, labelled diagrams with straight label lines and no shading; give the magnification where asked.
  • In practical questions, always mention controlled variables and repeating the experiment.
