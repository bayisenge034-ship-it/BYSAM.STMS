Teachers' Corner

Ready-to-use lesson plans, low-cost classroom activities, assessment tools and ideas for your own professional growth. Adapt everything to your class, syllabus and resources.

1. Lesson plans (40–45 minutes)

Physics – Newton's second law (F = ma)

ObjectiveStudents investigate how acceleration depends on force and mass, and calculate using F = ma.
MaterialsTrolley or toy car, string, hanging masses (e.g. 50 g), pulley or table edge, metre rule, stopwatch. Alternative: a student-pushed chair on a smooth floor.
Starter (5 min)Ask: “Which is harder to get moving, an empty or a loaded wheelbarrow? Why?” Collect answers with mini-whiteboards.
Main (25 min)Groups pull the trolley with 1, 2, then 3 masses and time a fixed distance; repeat with extra mass on the trolley. Teacher models a worked example (1200 kg car, 3000 N → 2.5 m/s²); students do three calculations.
Plenary (5–10 min)Exit ticket: “A 5 kg block gets 15 N net force. What is its acceleration?” (3 m/s²)
DifferentiationSupport: formula triangle and unit checklist. Extension: include friction, finding resultant force first.

Mathematics – Solving quadratics by factorising

ObjectiveStudents factorise x² + bx + c and solve the resulting equation.
MaterialsBoard, mini-whiteboards or exercise books, factor-pair cards (optional).
Starter (5 min)“Find two numbers with product 6 and sum 5.” Repeat with product 12, sum 7; product −12, sum 1.
Main (25 min)Model x² − 5x + 6 = 0 → (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0 → x = 2 or 3, with a substitution check. Pairs solve a ladder of 6 questions, increasing in difficulty (end with x² + x − 12 = 0).
Plenary (5–10 min)Each student writes one common mistake (e.g. forgetting the second root) and how to avoid it.
DifferentiationSupport: area-model (box) factorising. Extension: when does factorising fail? Introduce the quadratic formula and discriminant.

Chemistry – Introduction to the mole

ObjectiveStudents calculate molar mass and convert between mass and moles using n = m ÷ M.
MaterialsPeriodic table, balance (if available), small samples of salt, sugar, water; calculators.
Starter (5 min)Count analogy: a “dozen” is 12; a “mole” is a much bigger counting unit (6.02 × 10²³). Why do we not count atoms one by one?
Main (25 min)Work out M for H₂O (18), NaCl (58.5), CO₂ (44). Weigh 58.5 g of salt if possible to show “one mole”. Worked example: 11 g CO₂ → 0.25 mol. Students complete five conversions.
Plenary (5–10 min)True/false cards: “18 g of water is one mole” (true); “2 moles of CO₂ weigh 44 g” (false – 88 g).
DifferentiationSupport: three-column table (mass, M, moles). Extension: add gas volume at r.t.p. (n × 24 dm³).

Biology – Osmosis in potato tissue

ObjectiveStudents plan, carry out and evaluate an investigation into osmosis and use percentage change in mass.
MaterialsPotato, knife/cork borer, ruler, balance, sugar or salt, beakers, water, paper towels, labels.
Starter (5 min)Show a fresh and a wilted leaf. Ask: “What has changed inside the cells?”
Main (25 min)Prepare identical potato pieces and weigh. Place in four solutions (0, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0 mol/dm³). Leave 30+ minutes (or overnight), blot and reweigh. Calculate % change = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100.
Plenary (5–10 min)Explain results using “water potential”. Identify two controlled variables and one source of error.
DifferentiationSupport: pre-printed results table. Extension: graph % change vs concentration and estimate the isotonic point.

2. Classroom activities (low or no cost)

  • Think–Pair–Share: pose a question, give 1 minute of silent thought, 2 minutes with a partner, then share with the class.
  • Mini-whiteboards or slates: every student writes an answer and holds it up – instant whole-class feedback.
  • Human circuit (Physics): students pass “charge” tokens around a loop; a student with the larger step represents resistance.
  • Particle role-play (Chemistry): students act as particles in solids, liquids and gases, then show what changes on heating.
  • Genetics with coins (Biology): flip two coins as alleles to model Tt × Tt crosses and tally 25 results to compare with 3 : 1.
  • Error hunt (Mathematics): give a worked solution containing one mistake; students find and fix it.
  • Question swap: students write exam-style questions with marking schemes and swap with another group.

Writing a good marking scheme

  1. Break each question into separate, creditable points; award one mark per point.
  2. Distinguish method (M) and accuracy (A) marks for calculations, and note “error carried forward”.
  3. List acceptable alternative answers and common wrong answers you will not accept.
  4. Test the scheme: have a colleague mark five scripts and compare totals.

Question starters by difficulty

  • Recall: State… Name… Define…
  • Apply: Calculate… Use the data to…
  • Analyse: Compare… Explain why… Suggest a reason…
  • Evaluate: Judge… Which method is better and why?

4. Professional development

  • Reflective practice: after each lesson note one thing that worked, one that did not, and one change to make. Review these notes monthly.
  • Peer observation: agree a focus (for example, questioning) with a colleague, observe each other for 20 minutes and give two strengths and one suggestion.
  • Formative assessment: use short checks for understanding – exit tickets, whiteboards, hinge questions – and adapt the next lesson to the results.
  • Subject learning groups: meet with teachers from your department or nearby schools to share practical ideas and low-cost equipment substitutes.
  • Using data: use the Analysis tab in BYSAM.STMS to find topics where many students lost marks, then re-teach those topics.
  • Student wellbeing and inclusion: plan support and extension tasks so every learner can take part.
